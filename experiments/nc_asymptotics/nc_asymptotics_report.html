Six asymptotic normalizing constants on load-independent closed networks

kt, le, ble, nrl, nrp and nre measured against exact convolution over a grid of queueing stations, job classes and populations. nre wins outright — three to four decades below every other method on log G and two on the throughput ratio — and it is the only one of the six that never blows up. It is also the slowest, by about 20x.

How the six rank

Every model is a closed product-form network with single-server load-independent queues, random demands L ~ U(0.1, 1) and no think time. Error is measured two ways: on log G(N) itself, and on the throughput ratio X​r = G(N − er) / G(N), which is what the solver actually reports and where an additive bias in log G cancels. Four exact identities behind the numbers

le and ble differ by a constant, and it is the published one

The gap between the two is −(M−1)(1 − log 2π / 2) to six decimals at every station count, and nothing else. So le's error growing to 1.6 at M = 20 is not the expansion degrading — it is the missing correction term, and ble is flat in M once it is restored.

That constant cancels in every mean value

Because the offset is additive in log G and independent of N, it divides out of G(N − er)/G(N). Across all 350 ratio models the two methods do not merely agree closely, they agree to floating point. Choosing le over ble changes only getProbNormConstAggr.

nrl is nrp minus the Laplace error of the logistic density

Both methods push the Norlund-Rice contour through a transform of the unit torus — a logit for nrl, a probit for nrp — then Laplace-approximate. At R = 1 the torus collapses (t − t̄ ≡ 0) and the integral reduces to the transform's own density, which integrates to one. Laplace is exact on a Gaussian, so nrp is exact there; on the logistic it is not, and the shortfall is a pure constant.

The Laplace step occasionally fails outright

nrl and nrp share a numerical Hessian, and on 4 of the 587 models it returns a curvature that puts log G up to eight nats above the truth — both methods, same models, always at R ≥ 2. That is not a large error, it is a wrong answer, and nothing in the returned value flags it. nre, which tilts the contour to the saddle instead, never exceeds 0.19 anywhere in the grid.

What nre costs, and why

Measured separately from the accuracy grid, because the host it ran on carries a load average near 80 and wall clock there says nothing. These are CPU seconds for one log G evaluation, best of three, single-threaded BLAS. Both methods spend 97–99% of that inside the load-dependent kernel pfqn_gld / pfqn_gldsingle, so the comparison reduces to two questions: what one kernel call costs, and how many each method makes.

nrlnre

nrlnre
Counts, not seconds. nrl is 4R²-R+1 exactly; nre is adaptive.

Per call they cost the same — within 20%, scaling as M1.05N1.9 for both, flat in R. So the entire runtime difference is the call count, and the call counts have completely different characters. nrl makes exactly 4R² − R + 1 of them — a numerical Hessian over R dimensions, three evaluations per diagonal entry and four per off-diagonal, plus one at the mode — and that count does not move with M or N. nre's is set by a Newton root find and is adaptive: 1, 36, 114, 244 at R = 1…4 here, and 27 to 75 across the R = 2 runs alone.

Two things follow. At one class nre is the faster method, by 4.8× (0.015s against 0.070s): the torus collapses to dimension zero, so it returns pfqn_gldsingle after a single call while nrl still builds a Hessian. It is also exact there and nrl is 0.12 off, so that case has no trade-off in it at all. From two classes up the ratio is roughly 2× and climbs with the class count — 1.8×, 2.6×, 3.2× at R = 2, 3, 4. The spread you see along the M and N sweeps (1.7× to 3.6×) is not a size effect: it is nre's iteration count moving, and it disappears once the cost is divided by calls.

06

Which to use

default
nre

Best on both metrics at every station count, class count and population in the grid, exact at R = 1, and the only method with no catastrophic case. Pay for it in time: ~20x ble at N = 50 and it grows with N, so it is a poor fit inside a fixed-point loop.

when speed matters
ble

Ratio error 1e-3 median, 1.9e-2 worst, flat in M, and about as cheap as the six get. The natural choice when the constant feeds mean values rather than being reported itself.

large N only
kt

The only method whose ratio error falls monotonically with population, from 7.8e-2 at N = 2 to 7.5e-4 at N = 50. Below N ≈ 10 it is the worst of the six on ratios; above it, competitive.

reporting log G only
le

Identical to ble for every mean value. Prefer it only when the published Cas17 eq. 34 form is what is wanted; on log G itself it is worse by (M−1)·0.081.

not on these models
nrp

Exact at R = 1, then 3.3e-1 on log G and 9e-2 on ratios at R = 3, with the shared Hessian failure. Its reason to exist is genuine load dependence, which this grid does not exercise.

not on these models
nrl

Strictly nrp shifted by 0.121, so it is behind nrp everywhere, including at R = 1 where nrp is exact and it is not. Its one advantage is a fixed 4R²−R+1 kernel budget (§05).

Method. kt, le and ble are called through pfqn_nc. nrl, nrp and nre are load-dependent evaluators, so they are driven the way SolverNC drives them on a load-independent model — pfqn_ncld with mu ≡ 1 — which was checked to reproduce SolverNC(model, 'nrl'|'nrp'|'nre') exactly. The reference pfqn_ca was checked against brute-force enumeration of the product-form state space to machine precision.

Two further blocks were run and are in summary.txt: a think-time block (Z = 5 per class) and a near-balanced-demand block (L = 1 ± 1%). Neither changes the ranking. The balanced block sharpens it: ble's ratio error falls to 2.5e-7 at M = 20 while kt stays at 8.7e-4, and nrl/nrp reach 3.0e-2 at R = 3. The think-time block produces the worst Norlund-Rice failure in the study, +8.0 nats at M = 2, R = 3, N = 50.
